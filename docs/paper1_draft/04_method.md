# 4. Method

## 4.1 Problem setup

We consider a state-based, one-step probabilistic world model learned from offline data. The data are transitions $(s_t, a_t, s_{t+1})$ collected by a behavior policy in a simulated environment. The model defines a predictive distribution $p_\theta(s_{t+1} \mid s_t, a_t)$. We evaluate the quality of this distribution as an uncertainty estimate, specifically its calibration: whether central predictive intervals of nominal coverage $p$ contain the realized next state with frequency $p$.

The object of study is a trained model together with an evaluation condition. A condition is either the baseline (no intervention) or one of three mechanism-specific interventions applied at evaluation time: dynamics (D), observation (O), or policy (P). The outcome $Y$ is a calibration error (Section 4.6). Let $Y_i(0)$ be the outcome for instance $i$ under the baseline and $Y_i(m)$ the outcome for the same instance under mechanism $m \in \{D, O, P\}$ at a given severity. The estimand is

```math
\mathrm{ATE}_m = \mathbb{E}\left[\, Y(m) - Y(0) \,\right],
```

estimated per training seed as the mean of paired differences over instances, then summarized across seeds. A positive ATE means the intervention increases calibration error. The causal language refers to this controlled computational design: the intervention is set by us, the model is fixed, and the paired baseline holds other factors constant. It does not refer to identification in a real-world system.

## 4.2 Model

The world model was a 5-member ensemble of probabilistic Gaussian MLPs. Each member took the concatenated observation and action as input, passed it through two hidden layers of 256 units with ReLU activations, and had two linear heads, `mu_head` and `logvar_head`, each of the form Linear(256, output_dim). The log-variance output was clamped to $[-10.0, 5.0]$. The mean used a residual form, $\mu = s_t + \Delta\mu$, where $\Delta\mu$ is the head output. The log-variance head had no residual.

Inputs and targets were standardized per dimension using the mean and standard deviation fitted on the training split only. Each member was trained to minimize the Gaussian negative log-likelihood, averaged over the batch and output dimensions:

```math
\mathcal{L}(\theta) = \frac{1}{B\,d}\sum_{b=1}^{B}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\left[\frac{(y_{bk}-\mu_{bk})^2}{2\sigma_{bk}^2} + \tfrac{1}{2}\log \sigma_{bk}^2\right] + \text{const}.
```

Members were trained independently. The ensemble predictive distribution for a given input was summarized by a mean $\mu = \frac{1}{5}\sum_j \mu_j$ and a total variance $\sigma^2 = \frac{1}{5}\sum_j \sigma_j^2 + \mathrm{Var}_j(\mu_j)$, the sum of the average member variance and the disagreement between member means. ## 4.3 Data splits

Splits were made at the episode level to avoid leakage between transitions of the same trajectory. Episodes were assigned deterministically to four roles: train (70%), calibration (10%), test (10%), and probe (10%). The split seed was $1000 + $ the training-seed index. After splitting, transitions were capped at 150,000 (train), 25,000 (calibration), 25,000 (test), and 10,000 (probe).

The roles are distinct. The train split fits the network weights and the standardization statistics. The calibration split is used only to fit the recalibration factor (Section 4.7.2). The test split is used for baseline evaluation and the probe split for fixed-state interventions. [VERIFY: confirm which of test and probe is used for each of D, O, P in the released code; update this sentence accordingly.] The calibration split is not used in training and not used in any reported test or probe evaluation.

## 4.4 Mechanism-specific interventions

All interventions were applied at evaluation time. No model was retrained.

**Observation (O).** We added Gaussian noise to the model input observation only:

```math
\tilde{s}_t = s_t + \varepsilon,\qquad \varepsilon \sim \mathcal{N}\!\left(0,\ \big(\mathrm{std}(s^{\text{test}}) + 10^{-6}\big)\cdot f\right),
```

where $f$ is the noise fraction, 0.05 (low) or 0.10 (high). Here `std` is the standard deviation of the test observations. [VERIFY: confirm that it is computed per dimension.] The prediction target $y$ was the same as in the baseline, so the intervention changes the input but not the target.

**Dynamics (D).** We scaled the MuJoCo body masses by a factor $c$, 0.95 (low) or 0.80 (high). For each evaluation instance we reconstructed the simulator state from the observation: `qpos[1:] = obs[:qpos_len]`, `qvel = obs[qpos_len:]`, and the x-position was set to 0 because it is not observable. For Hopper, `qpos_len` = 5 and `qvel = obs[5:11]`. For Walker2d, `qpos_len` = 8 and `qvel = obs[8:17]`. The target $y$ was generated by re-simulating one step under the scaled masses.

**Policy (P).** We used fixed probe states drawn from the probe split, with no free rollouts. The action was replaced by $a' = \mathrm{clip}(\kappa a,\ a_{\text{low}},\ a_{\text{high}})$, with scale $\kappa$ of 0.95 (low) or 0.80 (high). The target was generated by re-simulating one step under the new action.

Each intervention changed one mechanism at a time. We did not test combinations of mechanisms.

## 4.5 Counterfactual evaluation protocol

The counterfactual unit was a matched model-instance pair: the same trained ensemble (one training seed), the same evaluation instance, and shared randomness. Baseline and treated evaluations used identical instances. For the observation intervention, which involves stochastic noise, we used common random numbers: the noise draws were generated from a fixed random stream so that the paired baseline and treatment shared the same underlying draws, and the intervention scaled them. For D and P, the interventions were deterministic given the instance, so no stochastic draw needed to be shared. Fixed probe states were chosen before any action modification, so the P intervention changed only the action.

The effect for a seed was the mean of $Y_i(m) - Y_i(0)$ over matched instances. Seeds are the unit over which uncertainty about the effect is quantified, because the training seed is the source of independent replication in this design.

## 4.6 Primary metric: instance-level regression calibration error

For instance $i$ and nominal level $p$ in $\mathcal{P} = \{0.10, 0.20, \dots, 0.90\}$, let $I_{ik}(p)$ be the central predictive interval of nominal mass $p$ for output dimension $k$, and let

```math
c_i(p) = \frac{1}{d}\sum_{k=1}^{d}\mathbf{1}\!\left[y_{ik}\in I_{ik}(p)\right].
```

[VERIFY: confirm that the per-instance aggregation runs over output dimensions in this way.] The instance-level calibration error is

```math
\mathrm{CE}_i = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{P}|}\sum_{p\in\mathcal{P}}\left|\,c_i(p) - p\,\right|,\qquad \mathrm{CE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{CE}_i .
```

We did not use the classification ECE. That metric bins predicted confidences of discrete labels. A continuous regression output has no analogous single confidence, and the regression notion of calibration compares nominal and empirical interval coverage directly [REF: Kuleshov et al. 2018].

The instance-level metric differs from a macro-average that first computes empirical coverage over all instances at each level and then takes $|\hat{c}(p) - p|$. By Jensen's inequality, $\frac{1}{N}\sum_i |c_i(p) - p| \ge |\frac{1}{N}\sum_i c_i(p) - p|$, so the instance-level value is never smaller than the macro-average. The two quantities coincide only if all $c_i(p) - p$ share a sign. Instance-level values are therefore not directly comparable with macro-averaged values from other work.

Because the metric is unsigned, it does not show the direction of miscalibration. We therefore also report signed coverage, $\hat{c}(p) - p$, where $\hat{c}(p)$ is the mean empirical coverage, and we use it in Section 6.2.

Secondary metrics were negative log-likelihood (NLL), empirical coverage at the 50% and 90% levels, sharpness at the 50% and 90% levels (interval width), and the continuous ranked probability score (CRPS).

## 4.7 Corrections

Two corrections were applied. They were identified from diagnostic analyses of the pipeline, not specified in advance, and we report results with and without the second.

### 4.7.1 Re-simulation bias

An identity intervention (mass scale 1.0 for D, action scale 1.0 for P) should leave calibration unchanged. We call the paired ATE of an identity intervention the null ATE. Using the dataset target as the baseline, the null ATE was not zero: approximately −0.0032 per seed for Hopper (SD ≈ 0.0005) and approximately −0.0066 for Walker2d (SD ≈ 0.0002). Two causes were identified. MuJoCo `set_state` followed by `step` does not reset the internal `qacc_warmstart`, and setting x = 0 in the reconstruction from the observation introduces a systematic offset. Both make re-simulated targets differ slightly from the dataset targets.

The O intervention only modifies the input, and its target is the dataset target, so it is not affected. For D and P, we used the re-simulated target at scale 1.0, $y_{\text{resim}}(1.0)$, as the paired baseline target. The baseline and treated evaluations then share the same re-simulation pipeline, and the bias cancels in the paired difference.

### 4.7.2 Recalibration of the baseline

The trained ensembles over-covered at every nominal level (Section 6.2). Since a baseline that is far from calibrated can change how an intervention affects an unsigned error, we fitted a single variance scaling factor $\alpha$ per training seed on the calibration split such that empirical coverage at the nominal 0.90 level equaled 0.90. The recalibrated variance was

```math
\sigma^2_{\text{new}} = \alpha\,\sigma^2_{\text{old}} .
```

A value $\alpha < 1$ narrows the intervals. The recalibration targets one level only and applies one scalar per seed, so it does not guarantee calibration at other levels. The factor was fitted on the calibration split and applied unchanged to test and probe evaluations, including the treated conditions, so recalibration does not use information from the intervention. We verified that $\alpha$ fitted on the calibration split differed from $\alpha$ fitted on the test split for each seed, which indicates that the two splits were not interchangeable.

We present the pre-recalibration analysis (corrected for re-simulation bias only) and the post-recalibration analysis. The latter is the main result, because it compares interventions against a baseline whose intervals are closer to nominal.

## 4.8 Statistical analysis

For each condition, the seed-level ATEs were summarized by their mean and sample standard deviation. The 95% confidence interval was a Student-$t$ interval with $n-1$ degrees of freedom: $\bar{\tau} \pm t_{0.975,\,n-1}\, s/\sqrt{n}$, with $t_{\text{crit}} = 2.262$ for $n = 10$ and 2.776 for $n = 5$. Effect sizes and intervals are reported before any $p$-value.

For hypothesis tests we used a paired sign-flip permutation test on the seed-level differences. For $n$ seeds, the exact test has $2^n$ equally likely sign assignments, so the smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is $2/2^n$: 0.0625 at $n = 5$ and 0.00195 at $n = 10$. At $n = 5$ the test therefore cannot reach $p < 0.05$ even when all seed effects share a sign, and any multiplicity correction makes this stricter. We used the Holm step-down correction over a family of six hypotheses (three mechanisms by two severities) within each environment. For this reason, we base conclusions on effect sizes and on the consistency of sign across seeds, and treat $p$-values as supporting evidence.
