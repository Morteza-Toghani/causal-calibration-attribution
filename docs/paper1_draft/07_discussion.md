# 7. Discussion

## 7.1 Interpretation: observation shift as the dominant pathway

After baseline recalibration, observation noise produced the largest change in calibration error in both environments. The observation ATEs were between +0.07955 and +0.26588, whereas dynamics and policy effects were at most 0.01111 in magnitude. A plausible reading is that the three interventions perturb different parts of the prediction problem. Observation noise perturbs the model's input directly, so the predicted mean moves away from the target while the predicted variance has no way of anticipating it. The dynamics and policy interventions at the tested severities change the target through a single simulator step from states and actions that remain close to the training distribution. We did not test this explanation directly. For example, we did not measure how far the perturbed inputs lie from the training data or how much the member means disagree under each intervention, and we make no claim that input distance alone accounts for the ranking.

The ranking is conditional on the severity settings. The chosen noise fractions (0.05, 0.10), mass scales (0.95, 0.80), and action scales (0.95, 0.80) are not equivalent in any common unit. A larger mass change or a larger action change may produce larger effects, and we did not map dose–response curves beyond two levels. The result says that observation noise at 5% and 10% of the observation standard deviation matters much more than mass reductions of 5% and 20% or action reductions of 5% and 20%. It does not say that observation shift is intrinsically more damaging than the other mechanisms at every severity.

## 7.2 Why baseline recalibration changes the picture

The trained ensembles were over-dispersed, with variance about 4.2 times too large in Hopper and 2.7 times in Walker2d. For such a baseline, any intervention that increases prediction error widens the gap between error and the stated uncertainty, and the unsigned calibration error can decrease before it increases, because empirical coverage moves from above nominal toward nominal. Recalibration removes most of this initial surplus at the 0.90 level, so that increased error shows up as increased calibration error.

The data fit this account in the following respects. Before recalibration, observation effects were negative at low severity in both environments, and the dynamics effects were negative in both. After recalibration, observation effects were positive and large, and dynamics effects shrank in magnitude. We note that magnitude ordering was already led by observation shift in the corrected analysis (for example, 0.03256 vs 0.02407 for Walker2d at high severity), so recalibration did not reorder the mechanisms by magnitude. What changed was the sign of some observation effects and the size of the gap: the observation effect exceeded the dynamics effect by factors of 14 to 178 after recalibration, and by a much smaller margin in the Walker2d high-severity case before it. The recalibration used one scalar at one nominal level, so it did not remove miscalibration at other levels, and we did not test more flexible recalibration.

## 7.3 Why `observation_high` changes sign without recalibration

Without recalibration, `observation_high` was positive in Hopper (+0.04129) and negative in Walker2d (−0.03256). Under the account in Section 7.2, the sign depends on where the baseline sits relative to nominal coverage and on how far the noise moves empirical coverage. If noise lowers coverage from above nominal, the unsigned error falls until coverage reaches nominal and rises beyond it. The Hopper high-severity condition would then be on the far side of nominal, and the Walker2d condition on the near side. The Hopper baseline's signed coverage was larger than that of Walker2d (+0.35 at the 0.50 level against +0.10 to +0.28), which would argue for the opposite crossing pattern if only the starting point mattered. The recalibrated results suggest that the observation noise has a larger effect on Hopper than on Walker2d (0.26588 vs 0.15449 at high severity), and this larger displacement may explain the crossing. We did not run a decomposition that would separate the two contributions, and we regard this explanation as a hypothesis. What the data show is the sign pattern in Table 6.8, and that it disappears after recalibration.

## 7.4 Implications for prior work

Studies that report calibration failure under distribution shift typically compare calibration error before and after shift, using whatever baseline the model has. Our results indicate that two choices can change the reported effect. First, if the baseline is over-dispersed, the sign and size of the unsigned effect can depend on the baseline's departure from nominal. Second, if the intervention requires re-simulation, a bias of the order of 0.003 to 0.007 can enter a paired comparison, comparable in size to some dynamics and policy effects here. Reports that omit a signed baseline analysis or a null control cannot be checked for either issue. We do not claim that earlier studies are wrong, because we did not examine their pipelines. We suggest that the signed coverage of the baseline and a null control should be reported alongside shift effects.

## 7.5 Implications for uncertainty-aware decision-making

Planners that use ensemble uncertainty as a penalty or as a trust signal assume that stated uncertainty tracks error [REF: Yu et al., MOPO; Kidambi et al., MOReL]. In our setting, this assumption failed most under observation noise, even after the baseline was recalibrated. If this pattern holds in deployed systems, then guarding against sensor noise or state-estimation error may matter more for the reliability of the model's uncertainty than guarding against moderate mass changes or moderate action-scale changes. This is a conditional statement about the tested severities and a one-step prediction task. We did not evaluate multi-step rollouts, planning performance, or safety outcomes, and we did not test whether recalibrated uncertainty improves downstream decisions.

The negative dynamics effects also deserve care in decision-making contexts. A lower unsigned calibration error under dynamics shift does not imply better predictions, and a planner that relies on calibrated intervals may behave differently from one that relies on accuracy. We did not test this.

## 7.6 Relation to identification assumptions

The design is a controlled computational intervention. The treatment is assigned by the analyst, the trained model is fixed, the instances are matched, and the random draws are shared where noise is involved. Under these conditions, the paired difference estimates the effect of the intervention on the metric for the model and instances evaluated, with no unmeasured confounding by construction. Several assumptions remain. The intervention must act on the mechanism it is named for; we reconstruct simulator states from observations with x set to 0, and the bias this introduces is estimated by the null control but not eliminated for the treated conditions. The effects are conditional on the data split and on the trained seeds. The effect on the real system, where mass, sensor noise, and behavior shift jointly and where the shift is not chosen by the analyst, is not identified by this design. The word "causal" in this paper refers to the simulated intervention on a fixed model.
