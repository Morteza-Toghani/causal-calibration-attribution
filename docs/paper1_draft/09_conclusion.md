# 9. Conclusion

We asked which mechanism-specific distribution shift causally produces calibration failure in an uncertainty-aware offline world model, and by how much. We answered it with a controlled computational design. 5-member Gaussian MLP ensembles trained on Minari Hopper (n = 10 seeds) and Walker2d (n = 5 seeds) were evaluated under evaluation-time interventions on dynamics (body mass), observation (input noise), and policy (action scale), each at two severities. We used matched instances and common random numbers, and estimated the paired ATE on an instance-level regression calibration error.

Three findings stand out. First, the trained ensembles were over-dispersed at every nominal level. In Hopper, signed coverage at the 0.50 level was +0.35, and a per-seed variance factor fitted on a held-out calibration split (alpha = 0.2361 for Hopper and 0.3725 for Walker2d) indicated variances about 4.2 and 2.7 times too large. Second, after this recalibration, observation shift dominated in both environments, with ATEs between +0.07955 and +0.26588, whereas dynamics and policy effects did not exceed 0.01111 in magnitude. Before recalibration, the observation effect at high severity had opposite signs in the two environments (+0.04129 in Hopper, −0.03256 in Walker2d). Third, re-simulation in MuJoCo introduced a bias in identity interventions of about −0.0032 (Hopper) and −0.0066 (Walker2d), which we removed by using a re-simulated paired baseline. The naive hypothesis that every intervention increases calibration error was rejected: dynamics interventions reduced the metric in both environments.

The main implication is that attributing calibration failure to a shift mechanism depends on the calibration of the baseline and on the handling of simulator bias, and that reports without a signed baseline analysis and a null control may conflate mechanisms. Within the tested severities, input noise was the pathway to guard against first. These conclusions hold for the tested model class, two locomotion environments, one-step prediction, and unmatched severity levels, and they describe a simulated intervention on a fixed model, not a real-world causal effect.

Future work should test whether more expressive recalibration changes the effect sizes, whether the ranking holds across severities, model classes, and horizons, and whether recalibration informed by the dominant mechanism improves the safety of decisions made with these models.
